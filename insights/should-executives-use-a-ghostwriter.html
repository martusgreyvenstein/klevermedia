Should Executives Use a Ghostwriter for LinkedIn?

16 September 2026

The objection comes up in almost every first conversation: isn't it dishonest to have someone else write your posts? It's a fair question, and the honest answer is that it depends entirely on what the ghostwriter is actually doing.

The line isn't who types it, it's who thinks it

A ghostwriter who invents opinions you don't hold, or claims to know things you never said, is putting words in your mouth. That's the version people are right to distrust. A ghostwriter who sits with you, extracts the actual view you already have but never get around to writing down, and shapes it into something readable, is doing something closer to what a great speechwriter does for a leader who gives excellent speeches but doesn't have three hours a week to draft them.

The content is still yours. The thinking is still yours. What's outsourced is the mechanical act of turning a conversation into a finished paragraph, which is a real skill, but not the same thing as the substance.

How to tell if a post still sounds like you

Read it out loud. If it uses phrases you'd never actually say, refers to experiences you didn't have, or reaches a conclusion you don't actually hold, it's not ghostwriting done well, whatever the byline says. Good ghostwriting reads like a slightly more polished version of how you already talk, with your actual details in it: a real number, a real name, a moment from your actual week. That specificity is what makes it sound like you rather than a template with your name on top.

Where this tends to go wrong

Most bad executive content isn't bad because someone else wrote it. It's bad because the writer never sat down with the executive properly, and had to invent a point of view from a two-line brief. The fix isn't writing it yourself; it's making sure whoever writes it actually knows what you think.
